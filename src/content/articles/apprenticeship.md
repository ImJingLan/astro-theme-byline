In January we opened six apprenticeship positions for people without a conventional engineering background. In March we nearly cancelled the program. In October it became our primary hiring pipeline.

## Why we almost cancelled it

The first cohort struggled, and the reason was structural rather than personal. Apprentices were placed on teams with deadlines, and the people who were supposed to mentor them were the same people under the most pressure. Mentoring became the first thing to be dropped.

We had designed a program that depended on slack in a system that had none.

## What we changed

Three things, in order of impact:

1. **Mentors were given explicit time**, budgeted at four hours a week and visible on their calendars.
2. **Apprentices worked on real projects with a defined deliverable**, not on training exercises.
3. **Reviews happened every two weeks**, with a written note from both sides.

The fourth change was the one nobody expected: we stopped calling them apprentices internally. The word carried an assumption of juniority that made it harder for teams to treat their work as production work.

## The results

Five of six apprentices are now full-time engineers. The sixth left for a startup and we consider that a success too. Two of the five are now mentoring in the second cohort.

The program costs roughly the same as hiring two senior engineers through a recruiter, and it produces people who know our codebase, our review culture, and our users. We should have started four years ago.
